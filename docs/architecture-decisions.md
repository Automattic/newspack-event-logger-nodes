# Architecture Decisions

The load-bearing design decisions of the event logger. "Fixing" one usually reintroduces a bug already paid for. Each record states what forced the choice, what it costs, what was weighed against it, and the concrete condition that reopens it. A **Revisit if** is a sufficient tripwire, not the only door: an argument on the merits also reopens a decision.

Numbers are stable. Code comments, docblocks, [`AGENTS.md`](../AGENTS.md), [`docs/architecture-guide.md`](architecture-guide.md) and the rendered sheets cite a record as "decision N", so no record is ever renumbered: a record a later one replaces keeps its number and its heading, and opens with **Superseded by** that record. The substrate keeps [its own series](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md) under the name `ADR-N`, and a "substrate ADR-N" here links to that file.

## Decision 1: The stats schema: nine Ledgers, a key per scope

`Stats_Store` is the schema: which Ledger holds each measurement, the key and member a row carries there, and each Ledger's column order. [`flame-builder.tsl`](../topologies/flame-builder.tsl) declares the nine Ledgers, and `Stats_Store::LEDGER_COLUMNS` spells each declaration once in PHP, which `TopologyShapeTest` holds to the file. [decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them) says how a Ledger stores and ages its rows; this record says what the rows are. [The architecture guide's Stats Schema table](architecture-guide.md#stats-schema) lists every Ledger's key, member and columns.

![The stats schema. A row is [ t, k, x, columns ]: t the start of the five-minute bucket the request finished in, k a key naming a scope, x a member of it, and the columns the Ledger declares; the Ledger stores it as ( t, k, x, w, s, columns ), w the partition that wrote it and s a sequence of the writer's. A table gives each of the nine Ledgers its key, member and columns: stats:totals, key site, count, sum_ms, requests and sum_peak_mb; stats:dims, the dimension or {dim}:{server_key}, the value, count, sum_ms, sum_peak_mb and timed; stats:categories and stats:leaderboard, site or srv:{server_key}, the category, or for the leaderboard the empty member or a category and entry joined by a tab; stats:url-rows, r:{server_key} for reader traffic and w:{server_key} for worker traffic, the URL, nine sums and the extremes min_ms:min, max_ms:max, max_peak_mb:max and last_seen:max; stats:url-dims, {dim}:{url_key}, the value; stats:url-cats, the url_key, the category; and the two sets with no columns, stats:names, url:{hash} or servers:r and servers:w, and stats:search, a word, a URL, both filed at the hour's start. Three cards: server_key() is the FNV-1a hash of the server name, so a Host header puts no bytes of its own into a key; url_key() percent-encodes a URL's whitespace, since a key holds none; and the flame-stats:url Table, one per partition, holds each URL's flame blob under its hash, capped to ITEM_BUDGET, 900,000 bytes, by its producer.](img/dec-key-layout.png)

A row is `[ t, k, x, columns ]`. `t` is the start of the five-minute bucket the request finished in ([decision 19](#decision-19-a-request-is-filed-in-the-bucket-it-finished-in)), `k` names a scope, and `x` a member of it. A read names its scopes, so the scope rides in the key and the Ledger seeks it at each `t`: a chart asks one key over 288 buckets, a URL page two keys a server, and a breakdown one dimension of one URL as one key (`Stats_Store::url_dim_key()`). `k` holds no whitespace, so a server rides in it as `Stats_Store::server_key()`, the FNV-1a hash of its name, which puts none of a Host header's own bytes into a key, and a URL as `Stats_Store::url_key()`, its whitespace percent-encoded. A member of two parts, a leaderboard category and one of its entries, is `Stats_Store::member()`, the two joined by a tab.

**Per-server data takes the server in the key**: `{dim}:{server_key}` in `stats:dims`, `srv:{server_key}` in `stats:categories` and `stats:leaderboard`, and `r:{server_key}` or `w:{server_key}` in `stats:url-rows`, one family for reader traffic and one for worker traffic. A site-wide scope is `site`, or the bare dimension. **A row carries nothing its key implies**: a URL row's member is its URL and its columns name no server. A row files under the URL's own host, and [decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them) caps how many servers a window names.

**The keys carry hashes, so `stats:names` turns them back.** `url:{hash}` names the URL a hash stands for, and `servers:r` and `servers:w` each server that filed rows of a family, each filed at its hour's start. `Stats_Store::url_of()` resolves a `dump_url`, `url_breakdown` or `url:` hash through it, and `Stats_Store::servers()` names the servers a site page reads.

**A URL's flame blob is not a row.** The `flame-stats:url` Table holds it, one Table per partition, under its bare hash, for `<eln:stats_url_ttl>` seconds, a twenty-fourth of the window floored at an hour. Its producer caps it to `Stats_Store::ITEM_BUDGET` (900,000 bytes, uncompressed) before it writes: the profiles to half, through the leaderboard's `MAX_LB_CATEGORIES` (200) and a byte estimate `Stats_Store::overhead()` makes for the serializer `Durable_Arm::serializer()` names, and each of the two flame-tree copies to a quarter (`Flame_Tree::prune_lightest()`). `Rule_Set`'s hook list rides memcached through an `auto` Table and caps to the same budget, which is why it sits under memcached's 1,048,576-byte item.

*Alternatives rejected:* a timestamp inside `k`, which `t` already carries: the primary key leads with `t`, so a settle's rows sit together whatever `k` holds; a server's name in the key, which lets a Host header write its own bytes into every key; one Ledger for every measurement, which gives every row the union of the columns.

**Revisit if** a read needs a scope no key names: the answer is a new key shape in the Ledger that holds the measurement, not a filter over its members.

## Decision 2: Sums, not means

Every stored measurement is a raw sum — a leaderboard row's `samples`, `sum_time` and `sum_count`, a URL row's `count`, `sum_ms` and `timed_count`, a profile's `count` and `sum_req_time` — so cross-bucket and cross-partition merges are exact addition and the display layer computes means at read time. A URL's blob follows the same rule: each flame-builder partition writes the share of the URL's traffic it saw, and `Stats_Store::url_stats()` adds every partition's running flame and profile before dividing once, because the first partition's blob alone answered for a quarter of a four-partition URL. Do NOT regress to running-mean storage: it produces the EMA-clamp bug this rule exists to keep out.

## Decision 3: Stats fail soft

Every read [`Stats_Store`](../includes/class-stats-store.php) makes fails soft. A Ledger or url Table that refuses or does not answer reads as empty, the store remembers it (`Stats_Store::unanswered()`), and the reply says `provisional`, so a dashboard shows "no data" or a short page where a stat should be. The pages and charts carry that flag: `urls`, its header, `url_breakdown`. A verb whose answer names one URL — `dump_url`, the `url:` brief, and a `span:` or `category:` brief under a `url:` — refuses instead, naming the Ledger that did not answer, because a URL it cannot resolve is not a URL that is missing. A Ledger no active topology declares is no store and reads as empty too. A `provisional` page or header is served and never cached, so the first poll after the store answers reads it whole. A url Table whose backend cannot open, which the mount raises as `\Newspack_Nodes\Table_Unavailable`, reads as no blob and logs `performance: the url Tables did not mount`. Any other mount failure fails the verb loud: a mount in a process running as root, a store two topologies declare differently, a Ledger file another declaration made, or a directory the runtime refuses to adopt.

The writer fails soft the same way. An `APPEND` a Ledger refuses answers null, and the rows it carried are the span's loss, counted `refused {ledger}` on the `stats writes` line and never retried. A URL blob the Table did not answer is not held that span, so no settle writes one span's fold over the stored blob, and a blob write the Table refuses is counted `refused url blobs`. A failure that raises out of a settle fails the Consumer's checkpoint instead, so its cursor never commits past the records whose deltas were lost ([decision 37](#decision-37-the-span-settles-at-the-interval-checkpoint-and-every-other-checkpoint-carries-it)). **A writer never writes from a read a store left unanswered**: the one read a settle makes, the servers `stats:names` holds, which `admitted()` caps the span's servers against, throws when the Ledger does not answer, before anything is appended, so the checkpoint fails and the span stays carried. Admitting against an unanswered read would treat it as no server held and file up to `MAX_SERVER_VALUES` new ones by name.

The substrate makes the opposite call for a different outage: a stream its slot pool cannot account for is refused ([SSE host budget](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/sse-host-budget.md)). The asymmetry is the decision. A missing stat costs a blank card; a stream nobody can account for costs a php-fpm child for as long as it stays open.

## Decision 4: Settings fan-out is a node graph; no consumer means a silent no-op

The three settings a hub pushes, the ruleset, `log_memory` and `flush_every_line`, ride the substrate's settings control plane: [`hub-control.tsl`](../topologies/hub-control.tsl) includes `settings-sync` and adds one `add_setting` line per option, and a change to any of them is an option write like every other, which the substrate's [`Settings_Event_Writer`](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/v2.56.0/includes/class-settings-event-writer.php) records. So [`Auto_Tuner_Node`](../includes/class-auto-tuner-node.php) mutates the one rule the message names (`rule_id`) and persists the whole ruleset through `Rule_Set::save()`: no `remote_manager` job, no `suppress_sync`, and an auto-tune write reaches the spokes exactly as an admin edit does. Nothing fans a change out unless `hub-control` is active and per-spoke `HTTP_Out` nodes are wired; on a spoke or a standalone site the event is tailed and dropped. That IS the structural gate: there is no `enable_workers` or `enable_aggregator` key, and hub mode derives from which topologies are active.

## Decision 5: A stats schema change is migrated by the tables flush, and by nothing in this code

A stats schema change here is migrated by emptying the stats stores, and `Stats_Store` keeps no salt and no migration path of its own:

```bash
wp nodes tables flush stats:totals stats:dims stats:categories stats:leaderboard stats:url-rows stats:url-dims stats:url-cats stats:names stats:search flame-stats:url
```

Schema bumps and emergency flushes share that one command. A Ledger's flush drops its `rows` table and declares it anew from the topology in one transaction, never unlinking the file every partition holds open; each live worker declaring it flushes over its command channel, and with none live the CLI flushes the file itself. The url Table's flush replaces each partition's file. Neither store's key carries a salt, so the substrate's `wp nodes memcache flush`, over [`Cache_Backend::rotate_salt()`](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/v2.56.0/includes/class-cache-backend.php), reaches memcached and APCu alone and leaves the stats in place. **The tables flush IS the migration, and nothing in the code compensates for skipping it.** A column change cannot be skipped: a Ledger whose file another declaration made refuses to open, naming the flush. A change to what a key or a member means can be, and then the dashboard reads rows of two shapes until the old ones age out; that is an operator error with a one-command fix, not a case for the runtime to detect, so no reader or writer carries a shape probe, a version component or a row-level legacy test.

![The stats schema migration drawn on two stores. Left, memcached: a volatile Table stores Cache_Backend::entry_key(), a key carrying the salted install scope from Cache_Backend::site_key(), so wp nodes memcache flush runs Cache_Backend::rotate_salt(), which stores a new salt, the old key sits in its store until its TTL with nothing asking for it, and every worker is asked to restart because each memoizes the scope. Right, SQLite: a stats Ledger's rows sit in one file, {base}/ledgers/{name}.sqlite, and the url Table's blobs in one file a partition, {base}/tables/flame-stats:url.p{N}.sqlite, neither keyed with a salt, so the rotation leaves them untouched; wp nodes tables flush drops and redeclares each Ledger's rows table in one transaction, never unlinking the file, and replaces each url Table file, a live worker flushing its own over its command channel. Below: a Ledger whose file another declaration made refuses to open, naming the flush; skipping the flush after a change to a key's meaning reads rows of two shapes until the old ones age out, an operator error with a one-command fix; no reader or writer carries a shape probe, a version component or a row-level legacy test.](img/dec-salt-rotation.png)

*Alternatives rejected:* a schema-version component in every key, a permanent extra component for the orphaning the flush already does; detect-and-purge at boot, a pass over every row on every start. **Revisit when** a deploy can no longer be relied on to run the flush, at which point the mechanism to add is one that forces it, not one that tolerates its absence.

## Decision 6: A read is one ask per store it spans

Every reader asks each store it reads once per question, and the Ledger aggregates in SQL ([decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them)): a chart is one `SUM` over its 288 buckets, a URL page one `TOP`, its header one `TOP` and two `SUM`s, a search one `MEMBERS` a word and one `SUM` of its candidates' rows, and a URL's blob one `MGET` to each partition's url Table. No reader sums a scope in PHP, so no reply grows with the rows the window holds. A read per key is the latency cliff this avoids: a message each, and on SQLite a statement each.

## Decision 7: Job_Intake for >4KB jobs, the firehose for ≤4KB

Small runtime jobs ride the firehose with `k:"job"`, and [`Job_Router_Node`](../includes/class-job-router-node.php) extracts them; [`job-feed.tsl`](../topologies/job-feed.tsl) tails the substrate's `jobfeed` log instead, so a spoke's router reads jobs alone rather than the whole firehose. A job the firehose cannot carry goes straight to `jobintake` through the substrate's [`Job_Intake::queue()`](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/v2.56.0/includes/class-job-intake.php), the locked path past the PIPE_BUF cap ([substrate ADR-4](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-4-pipe_buf-atomic-writes)). The wrong path loses jobs: `Log_Manager` trims `m` on any ENTRY over `MAX_DATA_SIZE` (3840B), so the handler never sees a parseable payload.

## Decision 8: A spoke's job is re-keyed `remote_job` when the hub pulls it

A spoke writes `k:"job"` like any producer and never learns it has a hub. The hub's [`aggregator`](../topologies/aggregator.tsl) topology pulls each spoke's firehose through a substrate [`Remote_Source_Node`](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/v2.56.0/includes/class-remote-source-node.php) ([hub and spoke](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/hub-and-spoke.md)), and this plugin's [`Remote_Job_Rewrite_Node`](../includes/class-remote-job-rewrite-node.php), wired between the sources and the firehose `Topic`, flips the incoming `k:"job"` to `k:"remote_job"` and leaves every other key alone. That kind is the only thing telling a spoke's work apart from the hub's own: the hub's `Job_Worker_Node` dispatches it against `newspack_nodes/remote_job_handlers` and never against the map its own jobs use. A job type registers under either map or both, depending on where it should run, and the two registrations are independent, so the hub runs a spoke's job because a handler was registered for it there, not because the spoke asked. What makes a site a hub is [decision 4](#decision-4-settings-fan-out-is-a-node-graph-no-consumer-means-a-silent-no-op)'s: the `aggregator` and `hub-control` topologies being active, with no toggle.

## Decision 9: A request's partition is its id, hashed by the substrate

## Decision 12: A stat times the request; a flame value is a rendering artifact

Two numbers describe one request and they are not interchangeable. `duration_ms` on the record is what the request took, measured once. A flame node's `value` is a LAYOUT WIDTH: `Flame_Tree::cover_children()`, `Flame_Fold::flatten()` and `Flame_Builder_Node::fill()` all RAISE it so a parent covers its positioned children, because the browser prunes on a single cutoff and takes a whole subtree with anything it drops, and `treemapDice` scales children by `parentWidth / parent.value`. Raising is correct for drawing and wrong for counting. **Every stat therefore reads `$request['duration_ms']`; nothing measured reads a flame `value`**: that covers every Ledger of [decision 1](#decision-1-the-stats-schema-nine-ledgers-a-key-per-scope) plus the per-URL aggregate's `sum_value`.

![A real request, the record and the raised root drawn to scale. The record's duration_ms is 356,312 ms, measured once, and every stat reads it. The two diverge by more than rounding: read from its raised root, a 356,312ms request reaches `avg_ms` as 724,662.87ms, because `fill()` raises the root to cover a child that ran 355 seconds after the parent's own occurrence. *Alternatives rejected:* not raising at all (deletes exactly the frames a viewer opened the graph to see); computing stats before the raise (the ordering is invisible at the call site, and one reorder reintroduces it). **Revisit if** the browser stops pruning on a single value cutoff: the raise can go then, and the two numbers converge.

## Decision 13: A sequence-break marker is missing detail, not idle time

`entries (lost)` and `entries (aggregated)` stand in for entries [`Request_Builder_Node`](../includes/class-request-builder-node.php) removed: discarded on overflow, or merged by the fold. It mints them, so it owns the vocabulary as `Request_Builder_Node::SEQUENCE_BREAK_KEYS`; consumers reference that constant rather than keeping their own copy, and the JS holds the only deliberate duplicate because it is a separate deploy unit. **No consumer may reason across a marker.** `Findings::entry_gap()` skips a gap on either side of one, `Reqgrep_Command` breaks its sequence there, and [`logEntryUtils`](../src/overview/utils/logEntryUtils.js) splices the merged spans back at it. Reading the interval as unexplained makes one record's `entry_gap` propose MORE hooks while its `truncation()` proposes fewer, both at once, on the same record. What a consumer MAY read across one is the record's own ending: whether anything closes the outermost pair. That decides FRAMING, which spans were severed and which ran to the end, and leaves the interval exactly as unmeasurable as it was, which is what this decision protects. `logEntryUtils`' `pruneSeveredSpans()` and `Reqgrep_Command::prune_severed_spans()` are the one rule, and the parity test in `logEntryUtils.test.js` pins the vocabulary they share. **The severance frames the record's RESUMED tail, never the folded interior spliced in at the marker**: those rows are what the TREE puts inside the open span, and the fold is what ate its close, so pruning before them makes every merged row a sibling of the parent it ran in: a gyrobase subprocess that exits 0 loses its whole 535s interior to the request's level, while the same shape whose drain survives the fold nests correctly. The prune therefore waits for the first row the record itself resumes with (`fromFold`). **Revisit if** a marker ever carries its own duration, which would make the interval measurable rather than merely missing.

One verb applies the filters, so one verb answers for what they left, and `overview` keeps only what the URL rows cannot answer.

The `urls` verb answers how many URLs, how many requests, the averages, the recent rate and the slowest ten, and, as its default page sorted by count descending, the busiest. The header and the table beneath it come from one reply and one scope and cannot disagree about what they describe; they may differ in AGE by up to `Stats_Store::URL_PAGE_REFRESH_S` (60 s), because each is cached apiece, the header once per scope for every sort and offset ([decision 28](#decision-28-a-url-page-ranks-in-the-ledger-and-a-search-reads-the-word-index)). The URL count is exact: a `TOP`'s total, or the size of a searched set. `as_of` names the page's build time. A filter matching nothing reads as an empty set rather than as zero traffic. The reply echoes the `filters` it applied (`server`, `search`, `errors_only`, `include_workers`), and the Ask brief carries them on every surface, so a narrower number never reads as the site's. Every rate divides by the WINDOW its recent buckets span — the current hour's closed buckets, or the hour before while none has closed (`recent_window()`) — dropping the bucket still filling, never by the buckets that happened to carry traffic: divide by those and a URL seen in two of twelve reads six times its rate beside a header that does not. `dump_url` takes the same `server`, because the modal opens from a row that filter scoped, and a site-wide average under a scoped count is the same defect one click in.

The first two are harmless because a URL carries its host, which makes one URL one site. **Revisit if** the header ever needs a number the URL rows cannot produce: the answer is a new field on the `urls` reply, not a second source read beside it.

## Decision 16: The breakdown panel is always mounted, and says which kind of nothing it has

[`BreakdownControls`](../src/overview/components/BreakdownControls.js) is the chart and the Metric and Breakdown dropdowns that drive it, plus a Server select when the caller passes `serverOptions` (the Overview card does): one panel, because the Metric and Breakdown values the chart reads are exactly the ones its own selects set. Both callers mount it unconditionally, and one resolver, [`breakdownState( breakdownData )`](../src/overview/AggregateTimeChart.js), which each caller runs once per reply, tells the panel which kind of nothing it has.

![The breakdown panel's callers and states. The dropdowns render in all four, because they are the only way out of a dimension with no rows, a read in flight or a refused reply, and there is no None breakdown to fall back to. A card shows what a gate does: falling back to a totals series draws a flat Total series under a dropdown reading User Agent, which is why overview carries none, and gating on the reply unmounts the only control that can recover from a refusal useCommandOnce treats as an answer.](img/dec-breakdown-state.png)

The dropdowns are the only way out of a dimension with no rows, a read still in flight or a refused reply, so any gate that can unmount them can trap an operator. A gate that falls back to a totals series when the selected dimension has no values draws a flat series legended "Total" under a dropdown reading "User Agent", which is why `overview` carries none; a gate on the reply unmounts, on an error, the only control that can recover from it, because `useCommandOnce` treats a refusal as an answer. The Overview card reads `overview.breakdowns[ dimension ]`, a map the server keys by every dimension it was ASKED for, so an absent KEY is a dropdown switch the reply has not caught up with. ## Decision 18: A stored value may be POSITIONAL, and then its indexes are named constants

A Ledger's columns are positional: an `APPEND` row carries them as a list in the order the `make_node` line declares, and a `SUM` or `TOP` answers them in that order. So three column orders are constants on `Stats_Store`, and a reader names a column by constant, never by a bare index: a URL row's (`ROW_*`), a dimension value's (`DIM_*`) and a category's (`CAT_*`).

![One URL row as its thirteen stats:url-rows columns, each named by a Stats_Store::ROW_ constant in the order flame-builder.tsl declares them: indexes 0 to 8 are the nine columns that add (COUNT, TIMED_COUNT, SUM_MS, SUM_PEAK_MB, the four status-class counts and ERRORS), which is ROW_SUMS, the one map that describes the row's summed half; 9 to 12 are the extremes and the last completion, MIN_MS a min column and MAX_MS, MAX_PEAK_MB and LAST_SEEN max columns, each null where nothing was measured. **The indexes are named constants, and a bare integer literal is forbidden**: the constants buy back the readability the shape spends, and `$row[3]` is unreadable AND silently mis-typeable, which is [substrate ADR-2](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-2-one-message-format-the-7-field-positional-array)'s own consequence in a second place. **The columns that ADD come first.** `ROW_SUMS` names indexes 0 to 8, the nine sums; the three extremes and the last completion follow at 9 to 12, `ROW_MIN_MS` a `min` column and `ROW_MAX_MS`, `ROW_MAX_PEAK_MB` and `ROW_LAST_SEEN` `max` columns, each null where nothing was measured ([decision 24](#decision-24-a-duration-nobody-measured-is-not-a-timing-sample)). So one map describes the row's summed half, and `Stats_Store::add_row()` sums two keys' rows through it without naming a field. Every `DIM_*` and `CAT_*` column adds.

**`ROW_FIELD_NAMES` is the one place an index becomes a name**, in the shape `Message::TYPE_NAMES` sets, and it spells each column as `LEDGER_COLUMNS` spells the declaration, so the topology and the reader cannot drift: `Stats_Store::url_row()` is the one boundary where a URL row's positions become the names the wire carries. The dimension and category series stay positional to the wire, `[ nameIndex, count, sumMs, sumPeakMb, timed ]` and `[ nameIndex, t, c, n ]` under a name table spelling each name once, because a chart's 288 buckets would otherwise spell every name 288 times; so neither has a field-name table, and `compact_dim_series()` and `compact_category_series()` are each series' one boundary. `DIM_*` takes the ROW block's vocabulary, `DIM_TIMED` beside `ROW_TIMED_COUNT`, because they are the same measurements: every request counts for volume, and an average divides the timed ones alone.

*Deliberately NOT swept in:* the leaderboard entry's `{time, count, samples}` triple in a URL blob's profiles (`LB_ENTRY_SUMS`), positional with no constants on both sides of the language boundary: a closed 3-tuple read by two adjacent functions and one React component, where naming it means naming it twice in two deploy units for three fields. *Alternatives rejected:* named columns on every row, which spell each name once per row where a Ledger declares it once; a per-Ledger index table, a lookup where a constant stands; a value object, which would have to serialize to something, and that something is this list. **Revisit if** a fourth column order wants constants, or when a tenth summed column is added to the URL row: indexes 0-8 are full, and a sum declared after the extremes quietly falsifies "the columns that add come first". `StatsStoreTest` asserts the four key sets are exactly `range( 0, 12 )`, `range( 0, 8 )`, `range( 0, 2 )` and `range( 0, 3 )`, and the url-rows declaration beside them, which is the mechanism that prose does not have.

## Decision 19: A request is filed in the bucket it FINISHED in

A stats row has to land where a reader will read it, so a request files under the bucket it FINISHED in.

![A request's stats row across a timeline. The request starts in one five-minute bucket and completes three buckets later, so min(now, timestamp + round(duration_ms / 1000)) files it at the completion's bucket rather than the one it started in; a skewed spoke clock or a bogus duration is clamped to now, because readers walk back from the wall and never reach a future bucket. For a timed-out request the completion is when live traffic timed it out: Request_Builder sets duration_ms = due - start at eviction, due the stream boundary its window fell due at, so the 1080-second assembly window bounds the exposure at eighteen minutes and a reprocess files it among the stamps it replayed. Beneath, a late record: it reaches the builder after its bucket and its hour have passed, as a spoke's backlog does on a hub, and the next settle appends it as one more delta row at its own t, which every read adds; no bucket closes and nothing folds. A row whose t is past the Ledger's lifespan drops at the Ledger, counted past the lifespan.](img/dec-bucket-filing.png)

The record reaches `Flame_Builder_Node` at completion, so filing it under `timestamp`, the START, puts a long request in a bucket the charts and the recent rate have already read as complete. The bucket is therefore `min( now, timestamp + round( duration_ms / 1000 ) )`: the completion needs no new field, and for a timed-out request it is when live traffic timed it out, because `Request_Builder` sets `duration_ms = due - start` at eviction: `due` is the grid boundary the request's window fell due at on `Request_Builder_Node::stream_clock()`, the newest entry stamp it has consumed, or the wall once it has consumed nothing for `Request_Builder_Node::IDLE_AFTER_SEC`. `LRU_Cache` hands each eviction that boundary, so a stream that falls silent overnight and repays three windows with its next stamp still times each request out at its own third boundary, not six hours long. That removes the request's own duration from the exposure, which the 1080 s assembly window, three windows of the same stream clock, bounds at eighteen minutes, and it holds for a reprocess run a day late, whose timed-out requests file among the stamps they were replayed with rather than in the wall's bucket. The completion is **clamped to now**: a completed request cannot have finished after it reached us, and a skewed spoke clock or a bogus duration filing into a future bucket, which readers walking back from now never reach, is the written-then-unreadable failure this decision exists to close.

**A late record needs nothing more.** Every settle appends its deltas at their own `t` ([decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them)), so a record that reaches the builder after its bucket, or its hour, has passed files one more delta row there, and every read adds it: no bucket closes and nothing folds. A hub sees late records with no replay at all, because one flame-builder partition takes every spoke's records and a spoke feeding a backlog files rows in buckets its caught-up peers left minutes ago; each lands where it belongs. The one bound is the Ledger's lifespan: a row whose `t` is already past it drops at the Ledger, counted `past the lifespan` on the `stats writes` line, because no reader reads that far back.

*Alternatives rejected:* filing by start, which changes a bucket after the dashboards read it as complete; filing by arrival, which loses when the request ran and files a replay's whole day in its own minute; no clamp, which lets a skewed clock file into a future bucket no reader reaches. **Revisit when** a record must reach a bucket older than the Ledger's lifespan, which the drop answers by counting it rather than storing it.

## Decision 20: Outbound HTTP is timed as a span, and a short-circuited request opens nothing

Without these spans a request that spends its time below PHP userland reports as unmeasured, the one answer a reader cannot act on. A BDN `wp-admin/post.php` record shows the cost: 41.7s wall against 12.2s CPU, with `Image_CDN::filter_the_content @999999` holding 29.4s of self time and no interior, so the 29.5s off-CPU remainder has no name. A hook cannot reach it, since `WP_Http::request()` is not a hook, but WordPress brackets the call: `pre_http_request` before, `http_api_debug` after. [`App\Core`](../includes/app/class-core.php) binds that pair for every log rule whose `log_http` is set, so the span costs two registrations per request and two entries per remote call. **The pair is NOT balanced, and that asymmetry is the whole design.**

![The outbound-HTTP span as a priority ladder. Step 1, apply_filters('pre_http_request', false, $args, $url): any filter may answer the request and return it, and http_start sits at PHP_INT_MAX, last to vote, so it opens nothing when $preempt is not false and otherwise pushes the label http and writes http (start). Step 2, the transport runs: the I/O below PHP userland the span exists to name. Step 3, do_action('http_api_debug'): http_end at PHP_INT_MIN hears it first, pops the label it opened rather than re-deriving it from a URL a redirect may have changed, and writes http (complete) with the status code, ahead of the other listeners. Beside it: WP_Http::request() answers a short-circuit with a bare return $pre; and never fires http_api_debug, so a span opened for it would never close and would adopt every row after it, the damage decision 13 bounds; the entry pair one remote call writes, m the redacted URL and l the calling frame; and the caller label, origin_frame(true), climbing past WP_Http at TRANSPORT_ORIGIN_DEPTH 16 rather than ORIGIN_DEPTH 8 whatever trace_callers says, with that budget reaching this span too, keyed on the redacted URL paired with the calling frame, so l names who asked on every call and caller carries the chain on the first few to each endpoint. Footer cards: the BDN wp-admin/post.php record, 41.7 s wall against 12.2 s CPU with 29.4 s of self time in Image_CDN::filter_the_content @999999 and a 29.5 s off-CPU remainder with no name; the cost, two registrations per request and two entries per remote call, under log_http; and every callback asking Log_Manager::started_instance() first so instrumentation never constructs the logger.](img/dec-http-span.png)

`WP_Http::request()` short-circuits with a bare `return $pre;` and never fires `http_api_debug` (`class-wp-http.php`), so a span opened before the short-circuit would never close and would adopt every row after it: the severed-span damage [decision 13](#decision-13-a-sequence-break-marker-is-missing-detail-not-idle-time) exists to bound. So `http_start` binds at **PHP_INT_MAX**, after every short-circuiting filter has voted, and opens nothing when `$preempt` is not false: a short-circuit is a cache hit with no I/O to time. A rule marking `http` significant gets that filter's listeners wrapped by `http_wrap`, bound at PHP_INT_MIN ahead of the chain on each call, the shape `hook_start` uses — `http_start` votes last, so from there a listener could only be wrapped for the NEXT call — so a short-circuiting listener's own cost shows where the span would have been. Every other return path fires the action, so within one request the pair is exact. `http_end` binds at PHP_INT_MIN so the span covers the request and not the other listeners on that action, and it closes the label it OPENED rather than one re-derived from the URL: a redirect hands the action the FINAL url, and a label that no longer matches leaves the span open. The span is named for its **caller**: `l` is `origin_frame( true )`, which climbs past every frame of the class that applied the filter, `WP_Http` here, at `TRANSPORT_ORIGIN_DEPTH` (16) rather than `ORIGIN_DEPTH` (8), because "which of my code paths makes this call, how many times" is the question a reader has; the redacted URL rides as the entry's value and the status code closes it. The climb runs whatever [decision 23](#decision-23-a-span-says-how-long-never-who-asked-two-knobs-answer-the-second-question-at-two-prices)'s `trace_callers` says, and taking the shallow walk under it is rejected: that budget reaches this span too, keyed on the redacted URL paired with the calling frame, so `l` names who asked on every call and `caller` carries the chain on the first few to each endpoint; the shallow walk would trade `l` for a microsecond or two. **Naming the HOST instead answers nothing**, because so many calls go to the same one. **Instrumentation never CONSTRUCTS the logger it reports into**: `Log_Manager::instance()` lazily creates a logger, so every callback asks `started_instance()` first, the wrapped listeners included; otherwise a binding that outlived its request would build the logger inside the callback and stamp `process (start)` with that moment instead of the mu-profiler's `request_ts`.

*Alternatives rejected:* a per-rule opt-in flag (a new `Rule` property, its serialization, its editor control and its migration, to gate two registrations and a per-call cost that is zero on the requests that make no remote calls); timing inside a `significant_events` wrapper on `http_api_debug` (that times the action's LISTENERS, which is not the request). **Revisit when** SQL wants the same treatment, though `$wpdb` offers no completion signal without `SAVEQUERIES`, so it is a different mechanism and not an extension of this one, or if a transport ever fires `http_api_debug` more than once for one request, which would pop a label that is still in flight. **Per-rule** (`log_http`), like decisions [22](#decision-22-query-spans-are-the-same-pair-as-decision-20-but-per-rule) and 24: a logged request pays two entries per outbound call whether or not a brief reads them. Off unless the rule sets it, as every diagnostic is, a stored rule that predates the flag included.

## Decision 21: The fold keeps the CLOSE of any span the kept head left open

The fold keeps a head of [`FOLD_KEEP_HEAD`](../includes/class-request-builder-node.php) (10), a rolling tail of `FOLD_KEEP_TAIL` (10) and an unbounded `keep` bucket, and a span opened in the head FRAMES every row after it, so the fold keeps the close of every span that head left open.

![One job record from community.thecoast.ca folded two ways. The gyrobase subprocess opened in the kept head (FOLD_KEEP_HEAD 10) and closed after 535.8 s of a 536.2 s request, and ten PHP rows followed it: nuclear_gyrobase, two query hook pairs, updated_option hook, memory, resources and the terminal. Without the rule, the close is one more row in the rolling tail (FOLD_KEEP_TAIL 10), the ten rows evict it, and the entry list leaves gyrobase open, so the view reconstructs framing the record does not state and three display defects follow, while the flame, counted only by Flame_Fold::close(), still reads gyrobase count 1, 535,829.753 ms. `is_kept()` argues that sizing the tail around a producer's shutdown sequence is guesswork the producer should settle by marking the line. A span's close is not that guess: the fold just chose the head, so it knows exactly which frames it left open, and there are at most `FOLD_KEEP_HEAD` of them. Nor is the close mere detail: losing a head span's `(complete)` costs the record its shape rather than one line. A `community.thecoast.ca` job shows it: the gyrobase subprocess closes after 535.8s of a 536.2s request, and the PHP parent's ten post-subprocess rows (`nuclear_gyrobase`, two `query hook` pairs, `updated_option hook`, memory, resources, terminal) fill the rolling tail and, without this rule, evict the close. The flame still counts the span, because `Flame_Fold::close()` is the only path that increments a node's count and the tree reads `gyrobase` count 1, 535,829.753ms; but the entry list leaves it open, and three separate display defects follow from the view reconstructing framing the record does not state. `open_in_head()` records the open bases at the fold and `closes_head_span()` routes their completes into `keep`, which the assembly splices between the `entries (aggregated)` marker and the tail. **The request's own frame is exempt**, exactly as it is for the display's severed-span prune: `process (complete)` is the terminal and must END the record, and `keep` lands BEFORE the tail.

*Alternatives rejected:* having the producer mark the line (`Gyrobase::Log` could set `keep` on its nested `complete('gyrobase', …)`, but that fixes one producer in another repo and leaves every other one to rediscover it); closing the span in the VIEW from the tree's count and value (puts derived data in the entry list, and leans on exactly the reasoning across a marker [decision 13](#decision-13-a-sequence-break-marker-is-missing-detail-not-idle-time) restricts). **Revisit if** a head ever legitimately leaves more frames open than the tail can afford to carry, which would mean `FOLD_KEEP_HEAD` is choosing a head the record cannot frame.

## Decision 22: Query spans are the same pair as decision 20, but PER-RULE

[Decision 20](#decision-20-outbound-http-is-timed-as-a-span-and-a-short-circuited-request-opens-nothing) leaves SQL as its reopen condition and calls it a different mechanism; it is the same shape with a different gate. `wpdb` brackets a query with `apply_filters( 'query', … )` before and, only under `SAVEQUERIES`, `log_query_custom_data` after. [`App\Core`](../includes/app/class-core.php) binds `query_start` on the [`query`](https://developer.wordpress.org/reference/hooks/query/) filter at the configured `hook_start_priority` (a plugin config key, default -10000), the priority the generic hook timer uses, so the span opens ahead of the filter chain and every filter that rewrites the SQL runs inside it. It records the label only, since the SQL at that point is what the caller wrote. `query_end` binds on `log_query_custom_data` at PHP_INT_MIN, pops the label and writes `sql (complete)` with the statement the database was asked: its host annotation stripped by `without_host_annotation()`, its literals replaced with `?` by `without_literals()`, and bounded only by `Log_Manager::MAX_DATA_SIZE`. A rule marking `sql` significant gets the `query` filter's per-callback timings nested inside the span, and with `log_queries` on, `query` itself is not bound as an ordinary hook.

![The query span as a priority ladder. Step 1, apply_filters('query', $sql) inside wpdb::query(): query_start at hook_start_priority (default -10000), the priority the generic hook timer uses, opens the sql span with its label only, since the SQL at that point is what the caller wrote, and every later filter that rewrites the SQL runs inside the span; a rule marking sql significant gets the query filter's per-callback timings nested inside the span, and query itself is not bound as an ordinary hook. Step 2, the database round trip, about 0.4 ms on the measured host. **The difference from HTTP is the cost, and it is measured, not assumed.** Both are per-rule opt-ins, but HTTP's price on a request making no remote calls is two `add_filter` calls. Queries cost two log entries EACH, and the close does not fire at all without `SAVEQUERIES`, so `log_queries` is the flag to weigh. What that constant costs was measured rather than feared: 3,000 queries run in 1.117s with it and 1.254s without, so the `get_caller()` backtrace is noise against a 0.4ms round trip, and what is real is retention, 217 bytes per query held in `$wpdb->queries`, the memory pressure that folds these records. `query_end()` therefore DRAINS that array, which is also why this cannot be always-on: anything else reading it (Query Monitor) finds it empty. `App\Core` defines `SAVEQUERIES` when it is absent, and a constant cannot be withdrawn, so a long-running worker keeps it for its life, bounded by the drain. **The span is named for its CALLER**: `l` is `origin_frame( true )`, as in decision 20, which takes the first non-machinery frame as the transport and climbs past every frame of it, whatever `trace_callers` says. A fixed skip cannot do that job: `wpdb::get_row()` reaches the `query` filter through `wpdb::query()`, and `SHOW FULL COLUMNS` arrives four `wpdb` frames deep, which is why the transport climb runs to `TRANSPORT_ORIGIN_DEPTH` (16) where a hook's own origin needs `ORIGIN_DEPTH` (8). Naming the TABLE instead says `SELECT wp_posts` ran 156 times and never which code ran it, and which code ran it is the only version of the question worth asking. *Alternatives rejected:* the `query` filter alone, closing each span at the NEXT query (charges every query with the PHP that ran after it); `$wpdb->num_queries` deltas on existing spans (free and always-on-safe, but counts are not spans, and the flame is what is being asked for); a `db.php` drop-in (accurate and SAVEQUERIES-free, but the host already ships one). **Revisit if** a reader needs the full callstack `log_query_custom_data` hands us rather than the one origin frame.

## Decision 23: A span says how long, never who asked. Two knobs answer the second question, at two prices

Decisions [20](#decision-20-outbound-http-is-timed-as-a-span-and-a-short-circuited-request-opens-nothing) and [22](#decision-22-query-spans-are-the-same-pair-as-decision-20-but-per-rule) add spans; this one adds PROVENANCE, because a hook that fires sixteen times reads as sixteen identical mysteries and no amount of timing distinguishes them. A `/wp-admin/post.php` record is the case: `the_content` fires sixteen times at ~2.4s each, 93% of a 40.6s request, and every span says the same thing. What is missing is the caller.

![Two provenance knobs at two prices, from a /wp-admin/post.php record where the_content fired sixteen times at about 2.4 s each, 93 percent of a 40.6 s request. Left, trace_hooks: origin_frame() takes one frame, who called the hook, and puts it on the entry's l, the flame's aggregation label, so one the_content node splits into a node per caller with measured durations and no tree code changes, and it never synthesizes an ancestor such as wp_trim_excerpt for an uninstrumented frame, which would assert a duration nobody measured; debug_backtrace(DEBUG_BACKTRACE_IGNORE_ARGS, ORIGIN_DEPTH) costs 0.9 microseconds against 12.7 for wp_debug_backtrace_summary(), the depth limit being the lever, so it runs on every firing, 2,601 render_block calls costing 2.3 ms. Right, trace_callers: caller_of() records wp_debug_backtrace_summary(self::class) onto the hook's own start entry as caller, not c, which means count throughout the schema; the rule's number is the count of deep chains, default 0, with a stored true reading as a count of 1, because the pair key makes one chain per caller a chain from every caller, and a strip of one CALLER's firings of one hook shows the first twenty carrying chains at trace_callers = 20 and the rest none, with a tick under the twentieth, the cap being per caller of a hook, not per hook and not per request; the window is CALLER_FRAMES, 20 frames, because a fixed 20 leaves 21 percent of a record unattributed and an 8-frame window stops one frame short of rest_preload_api_request. Footer: a field nothing renders does not exist, so LogEntriesTable renders the caller chain as called by …; both knobs are off by default, like log_queries and unlike log_http; and Log_Manager::message() merges arbitrary keys into the entry, [ 'n', 'k' ] + $data + [ 'ts' ], so neither needs a new wire field.](img/dec-provenance-knobs.png)

**`trace_hooks` is the cheap one and does the real work**: `origin_frame()` takes ONE frame, who called the hook, and puts it on the entry's `l`, which is the flame's aggregation label, so [`Flame_Tree`](../includes/class-flame-tree.php) and [`Flame_Fold`](../includes/class-flame-fold.php) split `the_content ×16` into one node per caller with MEASURED durations and no new tree code. Gyrobase uses the same idiom for `include: /Macros/Global.html`. It deliberately does NOT call `wp_debug_backtrace_summary()`, which walks and formats the whole stack to produce a string all but one frame of which is discarded: measured at **12.7us against 0.9us** for `debug_backtrace( DEBUG_BACKTRACE_IGNORE_ARGS, ORIGIN_DEPTH )`. The LIMIT is the lever, not `IGNORE_ARGS`: args are refcounted, not copied, and cost 0.8us even on a multi-megabyte `the_content`; depth is what costs. At 0.9us it runs on EVERY firing (2,601 `render_block` calls cost 2.3ms), which makes the split complete rather than truncated at a budget. **What it must NOT do is synthesise ANCESTOR nodes** for the uninstrumented frames: a `wp_trim_excerpt` parent valued at the `the_content` inside it would assert a duration nobody measured, which is [decision 12](#decision-12-a-stat-times-the-request-a-flame-value-is-a-rendering-artifact)'s whole point. Attribution is honest; invented ancestry is not.

**`trace_callers` is the expensive one**, default 0: `caller_of()` records `wp_debug_backtrace_summary( self::class )` onto that span's OWN start entry as `caller`, not `c`, which already means COUNT throughout this schema, ignoring this class so the top frame is the caller and not the instrumentation. **The cap is what makes it affordable, and it is per KEY, not per request** — a hook name, a query's statement shape or an outbound URL, whichever the flame aggregates that span on, PAIRED with `l`, the calling frame that span is already labelled by. A hook fires, a query shape runs and an endpoint is called for the same reason: code asked. Keying the budget per request instead would spend it on the bootstrap and trace none of the 477 taxonomy reads that came later. Keying it on the hook alone has the same failure one level down: `l` is what SPLITS the flame node, so the caller that fires first takes the whole allowance and every other node reads as an unexplained repeat — which is why the number had to be large enough to cover all of them at once. Paired, 1 reaches every caller, and that is the setting to reach for. The RULE names the number: `trace_callers` IS the count of DEEP chains, and a stored `true` is a count of 1, because the pair key makes one chain per caller a chain from every caller. What a diagnostic run wants is not what steady state wants: a fixed 20 leaves 21% of one real record's time unattributed. The window is `CALLER_FRAMES`, 20 frames, because an 8-frame window stops one frame short of `rest_preload_api_request`, the frame that explains that whole request, and `Log_Manager::MAX_DATA_SIZE` is the only other bound — which a query start entry meets differently from a hook's: it carries `l` and `caller` but no `m`, so `fit_data()` has no string to trim and the entry drops straight to the floor, keeping `n`, `k`, `ts` and a 1000-byte excerpt. The span still opens, because `Flame_Tree::PATTERN_START` reads `k`. The cap exists because the same question asked of `render_block` on that record would be 2,601 backtraces. Price the whole of it before raising either number: a traced rule pays roughly 12.7us per chain, times the number of KEYS it meets — hook names plus distinct statement shapes plus distinct URLs, none of which the operator declares — and the pair key multiplies that count by the distinct CALLERS of each, which is exactly why the number comes down as the key narrows: sixty shapes at twenty is about 15ms and five hundred is 127ms, where the same sixty shapes asked from three places each at 1 is about 2ms, and a traced query also pays `without_literals()` twice, once for the budget key at the open and once for the statement `m` at the close. A cap that bound tighter used to stop tracing the shape being hunted, which is why the answer was the per-key budget and a rule the operator turns off again rather than a smaller number; narrowing the key to the pair is what makes a smaller number safe, because 1 now buys one chain from EVERY caller instead of one from the first. The budget resets on every scope change, so a worker draining a hundred jobs traces each one rather than spending the request's allowance on the first. Sixteen firings of one hook are one budget PER CALLER — three callers, three chains at 1 — so there is no named default: `true` reads as 1, and an operator who wants more writes the number. There is nothing for a named default to seed, either: the editor fills its field from the stored value or 0, and `to_array()` writes an int. `Log_Manager::message()` merges arbitrary keys into the entry (`[ 'n', 'k' ] + $data + [ 'ts' ]`), so neither knob needs a new wire field. **A field nothing renders is a field that does not exist**, so `LogEntriesTable` renders `l` and then `caller`, as "called by …", muted ahead of the entry's value. Per-rule and off by default, like decision 22 and unlike 21: a backtrace is cheap once and not cheap always.

*Alternatives rejected:* capturing unconditionally for bound hooks (a rule naming sixty hooks pays sixty times per request for an answer nobody asked); tracing only firings AFTER the first, on the reading that a repeat is the signal (the first caller is half the answer: which of the sixteen is the odd one out is exactly what you are reading for); a separate `(called by)` entry (a new keyword that `logEntryUtils` and `Reqgrep_Core` would both have to learn, to carry a field the start entry already had room for). **This decision's revisit condition is MET.** It named a THIRD per-rule diagnostic knob as the trigger, on the reading that two booleans is a pair and three is a flags map. `Rule` carries five, `log_queries`, `log_http`, `log_plugin_loads`, `trace_hooks` and `trace_callers`, and they are still five separate constructor properties. A flags map would buy one property and cost every reader a lookup and every knob its type — `trace_callers` is an int, not a boolean — so the count alone is not the trigger it was taken for.

## Decision 24: A duration nobody measured is not a timing sample

[`Flame_Builder_Node::accumulate_all_stats()`](../includes/class-flame-builder-node.php) runs two independent gates, `$count_global` ([decision 15](#decision-15-the-urls-verb-owns-every-url-set-fact-overview-owns-the-site)) and `$record_timing`, and conflating them is the classic bug in that class. `$count_global` is "not a worker": it keeps worker traffic out of every site-wide aggregate. `$record_timing` is true only when `duration_ms` is positive AND `error_status` is neither `T` nor `A`. Both statuses mark a duration that measures elapsed time to an interruption rather than what the request cost: `Request_Builder_Node::evict_request()` stamps `T` and the stream boundary its window fell due at, less the start, on a trace the LRU evicted mid-request, and `Log_Manager` stamps `A` when a cooperative stop lands inside `finish()` or when `end_job_context()` is told the job never finished. Summing either skews `sum_ms`, `max_ms`, `min_ms` and every mean drawn from them.

![The two gates every completed request passes in accumulate_all_stats(). Gate 1, $count_global, is simply not a worker: it keeps worker traffic out of every site-wide aggregate while the URL rows keep worker timing in a w: family of their own. Gate 2, $record_timing, requires duration_ms above zero and an error_status that is neither T, which Request_Builder_Node::evict_request() stamps with the stream boundary its window fell due at minus start on a trace the LRU evicted, nor A, which Log_Manager stamps when a cooperative stop lands inside finish() or end_job_context() hears the job never finished. A table shows what every request adds and what only a timed request adds: on the per-URL row, ROW_COUNT and its count_Nxx column, ROW_SUM_PEAK_MB, ROW_MAX_PEAK_MB and ROW_LAST_SEEN always, and ROW_TIMED_COUNT, ROW_SUM_MS, ROW_MIN_MS and ROW_MAX_MS only when timed; on the per-URL flame aggregate, count always and sum_value with the children merge only when timed; on every dimension row, DIM_COUNT and DIM_SUM_PEAK_MB always and DIM_SUM_MS and DIM_TIMED only when timed; in stats:totals, non-worker only, requests and sum_peak_mb from every request and count and sum_ms only when timed; and the profiles, both leaderboards, the three category series and auto-tune, skipped whole. So count and timed_count diverge for a reason that has nothing to do with workers: mean_ms divides sum_ms by timed_count while avg_peak_mb divides by count, and stats:totals and every dimension row keep both counts, so each average divides the population it summed.](img/dec-timing-gate.png)

A request failing the timing gate still COUNTS: it increments `ROW_COUNT` and its `count_Nxx` column, `ROW_ERRORS` when it timed out ([decision 28](#decision-28-a-url-page-ranks-in-the-ledger-and-a-search-reads-the-word-index)), `ROW_SUM_PEAK_MB`, `ROW_MAX_PEAK_MB` and `ROW_LAST_SEEN` on the URL row, the `DIM_COUNT` and `DIM_SUM_PEAK_MB` of every dimension row, `stats:totals`' `requests` and `sum_peak_mb`, and `count` on the per-URL flame aggregate. It contributes nothing to `ROW_TIMED_COUNT`, `ROW_SUM_MS`, `ROW_MIN_MS`, `ROW_MAX_MS`, the aggregate's `sum_value` and children merge, the dimension rows' `DIM_SUM_MS` and `DIM_TIMED`, or `stats:totals`' `count` and `sum_ms`; and `accumulate_profiles()` is skipped whole, so both leaderboards, the three category series and auto-tune never see it. **So `count` and `timed_count` diverge for a reason that has nothing to do with workers**, since worker traffic files in a `w:` family of its own, which is why `Stats_Store::mean()` divides `sum_ms` by `timed_count` while `avg_peak_mb` divides by `count`. `stats:totals` and every dimension row keep the two populations apart the same way: `global_avg_ms` and a chart's average duration divide by the timed count (`count`, `DIM_TIMED`), and `global_avg_peak_mb` and the memory metric by every request (`requests`, `DIM_COUNT`). **The mean of nothing is null.** `Stats_Store::mean()` answers null for a zero divisor, so `avg_ms` is null where no timed request reached a URL row, the header totals, `global_avg_ms` or a board, and so is `avg_peak_mb` for a scope with no request at all. A `TOP` ranks such a URL last in both orders, as it ranks a `min_ms` or `max_ms` never measured: the Ledger's ratio divides by `NULLIF( denominator, 0 )` and orders `NULLS LAST`, and a searched page's `by_rank()` orders the same way. Every reader carries the null to its surface and shows it as unmeasured, never as 0, because a 0 reads as fast: a dash in a table or a brief, no bar on the URL table, and a gap in a chart, where a count or a sum of none stays a real 0. **Revisit if** an evicted or aborted request ever carries a duration someone measured, which would make it a sample like any other.

## Decision 25: Read tools and TUNE-scoped write tools share one MCP session, and every tool result is fenced

Every read tool returns recorded site traffic, and a visitor writes part of it. `dump_request` carries the `environment_v3` entry, whose `m` map is the curated `$_SERVER` block: `HTTP_USER_AGENT`, `HTTP_REFERER` and `HTTP_X_FORWARDED_FOR` are the visitor's own prose, capped at 256 bytes and stripped of control characters but otherwise verbatim. Serialized into a tool result with no delimiter, that prose reaches an agent's context indistinguishable from the server's own answer — and the same session may hold `rules_upsert` and `rules_delete`, so the agent it is talking to can edit the logging ruleset. What forced the decision is that pairing: recorded visitor text arriving unmarked beside a write tool.

One marking serves both exits, named once. A value recorded from site traffic leaves inside `<site-data>…</site-data>`, and the reader is told in standing text that whatever sits inside that tag is recorded data and never an instruction. `MCP_Controller::fence()` encodes every reply with `wp_json_encode( $reply, JSON_HEX_TAG | JSON_UNESCAPED_SLASHES )` and wraps the result, so a string reply encodes as a JSON string and the reader needs no second rule for it. `JSON_HEX_TAG` is what makes the fence unbreakable from inside: every `<` and `>` in the payload becomes `\u003C` or `\u003E`, so no visitor string can carry a literal `</site-data>` and close the tag early. The `initialize` reply's `instructions` field carries the standing text alongside the measurement caveat. An `isError` result is left alone, because that text is the site's own message rather than anything a visitor wrote. The browser's own exit, `src/overview/askBrief.js`, applies the same marking to the values a log record wrote — the subject URL and an entry's message — escaping `<` as `\u003C`, the spelling the MCP fence produces, collapsing control characters to one space and capping at 512 characters with a trailing `…`; both prompts it builds carry the same sentence. Rule ids, rule patterns, span names, hashes, rids and numbers stay bare, because the operator or the server wrote those. `Ask_Assembler::entry_shape()` ships the `environment_v3` entry with no body at all: its map is the visitor's headers and the peer, and the brief's `env` field already carries the allowlisted facts, so a second list over the map would only drift from the writer's.

Two alternatives were weighed and rejected. The first was a separate write-only session, so that no session holding a read tool could also edit the ruleset. That does not remove the injection, it only moves it: an agent holding both sessions reads the same recorded data out of one and writes through the other, and the operator now maintains two credentials for a boundary the agent crosses in a single turn. The second was dropping the write tools entirely. That would cost the surface its point — the ruleset is exactly what an agent investigating a slow URL needs to widen — for a defence the fence already provides more cheaply. The scope is subtractive and bounded by the minting user, so a `read` session offers no write tool at all and a `tune` session minted by someone who holds nothing does nothing; the operator chooses `tune` at mint time knowing the agent can then edit the logging ruleset and nothing beyond it, since the vault and the fleet are substrate `manage` and unreachable here. The fence plus the `initialize` instructions are the defence, and the blast radius stays recoverable: the ruleset is re-seeded from the config file and the schema through `Rule_Set::seed_from_config()`.

The cost is real. Every tool result is JSON, so an agent client that parsed a string reply as text has to strip the fence and decode; the hex-escaped angle brackets make a raw result harder for a human to read in a transcript; and the wrapper adds bytes to every reply. The marking is also advisory — it tells a reader what the bytes are, and a model that ignores standing instructions is not stopped by a tag. **Revisit if** MCP gains a transport-level way to label untrusted content, or a write tool appears whose blast radius is not recoverable from the config file, at which point the write half needs its own session after all.

## Decision 26: A merged transport node keeps the statements it ran

The fold replaces a request's entries with one node per path, and the statements go with them. A real `/wp-admin/post.php` record says `sql: WP_Query->get_posts` ran 667 times for 58.3s, and cannot say what any one of them was — the question a repeat that size raises. [Decision 22](#decision-22-query-spans-are-the-same-pair-as-decision-20-but-per-rule) argues the other way in as many words, that naming the TABLE "says `SELECT wp_posts` ran 156 times and never which code ran it, and which code ran it is the only version of the question worth asking." That holds for the SPAN'S NAME, and it still does: `l` is the caller, `Flame_Tree::node_name()` merges on it, and this decision does not touch it. What it revises is the unstated second half — that the statement is therefore worth nothing — which is true only while the entries survive to carry it. In a folded record they do not, and the caller alone cannot separate one query repeated 667 times from thirty different ones.

So a merged node whose span is one of the logger's own round trips — `Flame_Tree::is_transport_span()`, the `sql` and `http` states — carries `shapes`: the distinct statements or URLs it ran, each with a call count and summed milliseconds. **Only those two**, because their message is a bounded identifier by construction: `without_literals()` collapses `IN (?,?,?)` to `IN (?)` and every literal to `?`, and a redacted URL is one line. A hook's message is prose of any size and any cardinality, so it gets no table. **The identifier is the FIRST non-empty `m` of the pair**, because which half carries it is the producer's business and they disagree: `http_start` writes the URL and its complete the status code, `query_end` writes the statement and its start nothing. Reading the close alone builds a table of `200`s — and an `http` complete never stands in for a start a checkpoint lost, for the same reason.

Three bounds, and the third is the one that matters. A shape is cut to `MAX_SHAPE_BYTES` (512), measured against 308,395 real `sql` completes here — mean 146 bytes, median 122, p99 488 — so it truncates 0.6% of statements. A node keeps `MAX_SHAPES` (24) rows, one slot of which is reserved for the `…` bucket everything else folds into; one record in 184 had a node exceed it. A full table EVICTS its cheapest row rather than refusing the newcomer, because the statement worth naming is as likely to arrive twenty-fifth as first and first-seen-wins would bury it on exactly the record this exists for. **And the record itself is bounded, at `MAX_RECORD_SHAPE_BYTES` (32KB of new shapes), because the per-node caps bound a NODE and a record's node count is `O(distinct paths)` by design**: the heaviest real folded record here carries 117 transport nodes against 73KB of record, so per-node caps alone admit megabytes. The POOL sizes that number rather than the record: `Request_Builder_Node` holds 300 requests in flight against an entry budget worth ~18MB, so 32KB apiece keeps the worst case near half of it, and that heaviest record — whose own statements cost 33.7KB — is the one shape that spends its budget and buckets the tail. **The budget charges what the table GREW by**, never what a statement the bucket swallowed would have cost: charging the attempt let one high-cardinality caller spend 65,603 bytes to store 9,206 and strip every other node's table, measured at 7.1×. It charges a ROW's cost too, `SHAPE_ROW_BYTES` beside the key's own length, because a byte budget alone bounds bytes and not rows: at eight-byte shapes, which `without_literals()` readily produces, the rows cost twelve times what the budget thought it had allowed. A spent budget refuses a new row the table has ROOM for, and nothing else: a statement the node already keeps costs no bytes to count, and a full table's eviction trades one row for another and pays only the difference, which it may do while spent so long as the new key is no longer than the one it replaces — refusing every trade would bucket the worst statement on the one record shape that ever spends its budget, and admitting a growing one carried a record to 3.2× the budget, one filled table at a time. A folded envelope is also invisible to `Request_Builder_Node`'s pressure loop, which counts `entries` and skips a folded one, so nothing downstream would catch the growth.

The statement is recorded data, so it leaves through [decision 25](#decision-25-read-tools-and-tune-scoped-write-tools-share-one-mcp-session-and-every-tool-result-is-fenced)'s marking like any other: `askBrief.js` gives it a fenced line of its own rather than a slot in the `numbers:` list, which a reader splits on spaces and a statement carries plenty of.

`shape_ms` is a MEASURED sum, unlike the node's `value`, which [decision 12](#decision-12-a-stat-times-the-request-a-flame-value-is-a-rendering-artifact) calls a rendering artifact that every producer raises to cover its children. Nothing raises `shape_ms`, and nothing may: it is the time those statements took. The pair is positional per [decision 18](#decision-18-a-stored-value-may-be-positional-and-then-its-indexes-are-named-constants), under that decision's `LB_ENTRY_SUMS` exemption — a closed 2-tuple read by `Findings::worst_shape()`, `merge_shapes()` and one React function.

**An unfolded record gets the same table, derived rather than stored.** Its entries survive, so the log table shows every statement — but a brief ships sixty entries, so for any reader but the detail view the finding is the only place the answer can land. `Findings` folds the record's entries transiently through `Flame_Fold` and reads each node's table by its name path, which runs the one set of rules rather than a second, and adds nothing to the stored flame. That fold passes no record budget: the budget exists for a pool of in-flight envelopes, and a fold built, read and dropped holds nothing against it — keeping it would bucket a slow statement that first appears late and name a cheaper one.

*Alternatives rejected:* keeping ONE representative statement, the slowest instance's (costs no table, and answers "was it one query or thirty?" with a guess); a digest at the producer, in `App\Core::query_end` (the producer cannot know the record will fold, and a per-request digest would ride the firehose against `MAX_DATA_SIZE` on every request that never folds); the table on the per-URL aggregate (cardinality across thousands of requests is unbounded, and the aggregate's numbers are per-request means, which a summed pair is not — `merge_flame_children_incremental()` rebuilds each node from four keys, so it cannot leak there by accident). **Revisit if** a THIRD span kind earns a table, which would make "is it transport?" the wrong question and a producer-set marker on the entry the right one — `Log_Manager::message()` already merges arbitrary keys, and `Request_Builder_Node` already allowlists such fields beside `keep`. The sibling producers are the reason it would come up: pyrobase's `customquery_sql` and gyrobase's `query_sql`, `search_query` and `<tag> (sql)` spans all carry an already-shaped statement, and none of them is named `sql`.

## Decision 27: The platform's requests to itself are worker traffic, named by path

The cron loopback, the substrate's own endpoints — `/wp-json/newspack-nodes/v1/{auth,command,health/cache,log/stream,messages/stream,workers/spawn}` — and this plugin's MCP route, `/wp-json/newspack-event-logger-nodes/v1/mcp`, are requests the platform makes to itself, and until 0.101.0 the shipped seed skipped every one, so the logger never logged the logger. A skip loses the record: a `command` poll that took nine seconds, or a spawn that fataled, left nothing to read. So the seed is the `/` log rule alone, and [`Log_Manager::PLATFORM_WORKERS`](../includes/class-log-manager.php) names each of those requests a worker by its path — `cron` for the loopback, `restapi` for the seven REST endpoints — as the `worker_type` entry `log_worker()` writes right after `process (start)`. The seven share one name because the path already names each endpoint and `resolved_request_url()` appends the type as the URL's query, so a name per endpoint only repeated it: `…/workers/spawn?spawn`. The substrate's own processes name themselves through `NEWSPACK_NODES_WORKER_TYPE`, and that wins where it is set; the path answers where it is not, which for the spawn endpoint is always, since the substrate sets the var at REST dispatch, after the request was opened. So the spawn endpoint is the one `restapi` row that is renamed: once the worker `Spawn_Controller` validated holds its lock, the substrate fires `newspack_nodes/worker_identified` (2.71.0), and `Log_Manager::identify_worker()` writes `worker_type` and `worker_partition` again into the open record. `Request_Builder_Node` takes the latest of each, so a spawned worker's record and its in-flight gyroscope row sit on that worker's row, and existing `…/workers/spawn?restapi` rows stop accumulating and age out. A refused spawn, or one that loses the lock race to a live worker, never announces, so it keeps `restapi` and cannot mint a row out of the request's own parameters. A worker's record keeps every entry, sits in the worker family of the URL rows, and stays out of the global sums at [decision 24](#decision-24-a-duration-nobody-measured-is-not-a-timing-sample)'s `$count_global` gate.

**The REST paths are a copy the substrate does not know it owns.** `HTTP_In_Node`, `SSE_Out_Node`, `Log_Stream_Out_Node`, `Spawn_Controller`, `Auth_Controller` and `Health_Cache_Controller` declare those routes in newspack-nodes, and `MCP_Controller` this plugin's; `PLATFORM_WORKERS` spells them again as literals, root-relative and `wp-json`-prefixed, as the seed's skips did before it. They stay literals because `Spawn_Controller` and `Auth_Controller` name their routes inline, with no constant to read. `LogManagerTest` registers every route the platform serves and compares the set to the literals, so a route the substrate adds, moves or drops fails the suite. What no test reaches is chosen with open eyes: a subdirectory install or a site rewriting its REST base yields a request `platform_worker_type()` does not name, and it lands on the global rows as visitor traffic. That is a misclassification the overview would show, where the skip list's failure was a record that never existed. The map is not derived from `rest_url()` or `site_url()` because the seed never was, and because a copy that nothing checks costs one line per route where a derivation costs a `get_option()` per request.

*Alternatives rejected:* keeping the seed's skips (loses the record, and every stored ruleset already carries whatever skips its operator kept); the substrate stamping its own endpoints (its env var is set at dispatch, after `process (start)`, so the stamp would arrive too late for the entry that names the worker); a `Rule` property marking a rule's matches as worker traffic (a sixth knob, its serialization and its editor control, to say what a few literal paths say). **Revisit when** every substrate controller publishes its route path as a constant or filter this plugin can read, at which point the copy becomes a read, or when a platform request is seen in the overview's global totals, which is the symptom this decision accepts.

## Decision 28: A URL page ranks in the Ledger, and a search reads the word index

The dashboard asks for a page: the top rows of the URL set by one sort, or the rows whose path carries a term's words. `stats:url-rows` ranks the first in SQL and `stats:search` names the second's candidates ([decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them)), so no reader walks the URL set.

**A page is one `TOP`.** `Stats_Store::url_page()` ranks the scope's keys — each server's `r:` key, and its `w:` key beside it with `include_workers` — over the window, and answers the page and the size of the set from one snapshot. `count`, `min_ms`, `max_ms` and `last_updated` rank by a column's aggregate, `url` by the member itself, and `avg_ms` and `avg_peak_mb` by a ratio of two sums, `sum_ms` over `timed_count` and `sum_peak_mb` over `count` (`Performance_CI_Node::ORDER_BY`), so every sort ranks the window's own totals and every page is exact. A tie breaks by the URL, ascending in both directions. An untimed URL's `min_ms`, `max_ms` and `avg_ms` are null and rank last in either order ([decision 24](#decision-24-a-duration-nobody-measured-is-not-a-timing-sample)). The header is one `TOP` for the slowest ten and the URL count, one `SUM` grouped by key for the totals, and one more over the recent window for the rate. It is cached once per scope for `Stats_Store::URL_PAGE_REFRESH_S` (60 s), every sort and offset of the scope sharing it, and each page beside it for as long, both read through `Table_Node::backed_by()` in the `eln-urls-page` namespace with the page's field list in the key, since a read-through cannot report a stored value missing a field. A page wider than `URLS_PAGE_CACHE_MAX_ROWS` (250), or a `provisional` one, is built and never stored.

**`errors_only` is the keys and buckets in which a URL errored.** A URL's row, the totals and the slowest ten count the traffic of the stored rows in which it had a timeout or a fatal, and none of its clean traffic: the `TOP` and the `SUM` carry `positive: errors` with `positive_each_t`, so the Ledger drops every stored `( t, k, x )` row whose `errors` is not positive before it groups. A test of the window's merged row instead would count a URL's clean traffic with its errors: on eve 427 of the 505 requests of the URLs that errored ran in buckets where they did not. A key is one server's reader or worker family, so with `include_workers` a URL that errs as a reader and runs clean as a worker in one bucket counts its reader traffic alone. An errors-only `count` sort ranks the errors.

**An error is a timeout or a fatal, counted by its own column.** `Flame_Builder_Node::error_counts()` adds one to `ROW_ERRORS` for a record stamped `T` or `F`, whatever status it answered, beside `timing_counts()`, which it never reads: a fatal answers a 500 in a measured duration, so it is timed AND errored, and a timeout is errored and untimed. A status class cannot say it, because a fatal's 500 files under `count_5xx` and every CLI worker request answers status 0. An abort (`A`) is not an error: it is the logger's own worker stopping mid-request. Nor is a gap (`I`), which is missing detail ([decision 13](#decision-13-a-sequence-break-marker-is-missing-detail-not-idle-time)); `Request_Builder_Node` stamps it over a nominal finish only, so a fatal with a hole in its trace stays `F`. The URL modal's "Errors Only" keeps the same two, through `errorStatus()`'s `errored`.

**A search reads the word index, then its candidates' rows.** `Stats_Store::search_urls()` looks a term's words up in `stats:search`, `SEARCH_WORDS_READ` (3) at a time, longest first, since a longer word is the rarer, and intersects the URLs they name. A word filed for more than `URL_SEARCH_MAX` (5,000) URLs in the window narrows nothing, and the next three words are read only while none of the last narrowed. Every candidate is then checked against every word of the term on its own path (`Stats_Store::url_matches()`), a server scope keeps the URLs its own host logged, and one `SUM` of `stats:url-rows` reads the survivors' rows by member, which the page ranks in PHP as the Ledger ranks a `TOP` (`by_rank()`). A term no word of which narrows is refused, naming the limit and asking for another word; a term of no word names nothing. The name scan behind `Performance_CI_Node::$match_names`, which reads a scope's rows whole and keeps those whose path matches, stays off; it keeps its code and its tests, which turn it on.

**A search matches whole words, not prefixes or substrings.** `/wombat-7731` is found by `wombat` and by `7731`; `wom` and `mbat` find nothing, because the index files whole words, as `Stats_Store::term_tokens()` spells them: the path lowercased and split on every non-alphanumeric run, each run of `TERM_WORD_MIN` (2) characters or more, cut to `TERM_WORD_MAX` (12). A term's words are spelled the same way, and the search box's placeholder says so. Prefix-as-you-type is what it gives up, for an index under a third the size with half the words too common to narrow. Filing the eln staging hub's URLs three ways:

| Filing | Token sets | Entries | Uncompressed | Saturated | URLs unreachable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prefixes, 3–12 characters | 2.38 M | 37.9 M | ~1.10 GB | 503 | 3,629 |
| Whole words, 2+ characters | 826 k | 12.0 M | ~0.33 GB | 245 | 1,166 |
| Whole words, 3+ characters | 816 k | 9.7 M | ~0.28 GB | 165 | 3,455 |

Two-character words are filed because they cost ten thousand sets and reach 2,289 URLs no word of three characters reaches.

*What it costs:* a searched page ranks its candidates in PHP, up to `URL_SEARCH_MAX` rows, where an unsearched one ranks in the Ledger; a common word stops narrowing until fewer URLs in the window carry it; and nothing ranks at write time, so every read the page cache misses pays a `TOP` over the whole window.

*Alternatives rejected:* ranked lists the writer keeps per bucket and server, which a page merged, which ranked a sum approximately and cost every settle a ranking per server per bucket; a distinct-count sketch per key, which made the header's URL count an estimate; and a prefix index searched by a range scan, which brings prefix search back at 2.3 s at p99 over the staging hub's 15.9 M rows. **Revisit when** a word too common to narrow is the only word an operator can type, which answers every such search with a refusal and is the signal `URL_SEARCH_MAX` is sized wrong for that URL space; when the `url page cache` span shows a miss's `TOP` too slow for the cache to hide; or when prefix search is wanted back and its rows can be searched without a scan.

## Decision 29: Every reader dates from the tick, and a reply reads it once

Every stats reader dates from `Core::$now`, never from a wall read of its own. That tick is not still: `Core::right_now()` writes it, so every caller re-pins it, in a worker and in a request alike. One example from this plugin, not a list: the firehose logger re-pins it once per line `Log_Manager::message()` writes. Loading the substrate's `Core` class pins it first, through `Core::reset()`, so a verb has a tick before it runs. A reader wanting one instant reads it once and passes it down, and a test pins `Core::$now` directly. The flame builder reads it once per settle, in `settle()`, and hands that instant to `write_pending()`, which dates the window `admitted()` reads the held servers over, the `last_modified` each URL blob carries and the work mark `idle_since()` reports, and `settle()` stamps the settle mark `GET_STATS` ages from it, so an append that moves the tick in between changes none of them.

**So a reply reads that clock ONCE, at its entry, and passes it down.** Any `right_now()` caller can advance the tick while a reply runs, the firehose logger once per line. Each `Performance_CI_Node` verb reads `self::now()` in its handler and hands the instant to every reader of the window beneath it — `window()`, `chart_window()`, `recent_window()`, `chart_slots()`, the page and header cache keys and `as_of` — so one reply is one window: a page keyed under one bucket cannot be built from the next, and two panels of one overview cannot answer for different windows.

*What it costs:* a `$now` parameter on the private readers of `Performance_CI_Node` — `url_page()`, `url_header()`, `searched_page()`, `row()`, `scope_servers()` and the window helpers among them — which a test calling one must pass.

*Alternatives rejected:* a fresh wall read per reader (`\time()` or `Core::right_now()`), which lets a reply's panels straddle a bucket — `right_now()` also WRITES the tick, so a reader calling it moves the clock its own reply is dated from; a stamp pinned in `dispatch()` for the readers to consult, because a static stamp is a second clock with a lifetime of its own, which every caller and every test must remember to set, where a parameter makes the instant explicit at each call; and changing `Log_Manager::message()` to stop re-pinning, which leaves request scope with a tick frozen at class load and starves the debounce that reads it. **Revisit if** the substrate's tick ever holds still between drains, with no `right_now()` caller outside the drain in either scope, at which point `Core::$now` is itself one window and the parameter can go; or if a reply ever needs two instants on purpose, which would be a second parameter named for what it measures, never a second read.

## Decision 31: A snapshot node's replay is idempotent by the last folded crumb

A plain stop leaves the reader's cursor ON the record in flight, and the successor replays it (substrate [ADR-14](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-14-cooperative-stop-propagates-through-broad-catches)). Every stop but a bare clean one is plain: one carrying a failure, one combined with a failure, and one no `deferring()` bracket turned clean. The worker's shutdown checkpoint commits that cursor beside the span the snapshot node's `save_state()` carries unsettled, and that span often includes the very record the cursor still points at. A replay must therefore not fold it twice.

`Flame_Builder_Node` keeps `$counted`, the crumb — `Message::ID`, the consumer's `segment:offset:length` — of the last record it folded into its accumulators, and `save_state()` carries the crumb as `counted` beside the span it has not settled, both of which `restore_state()` reads back, so the successor's next interval checkpoint settles the span once. A carry lacking either key restores as a process that has folded nothing, which is a fresh process exactly: this is the defined state for a builder that never recorded one, not a migration. `restore_state()` does not refuse such a carry, because the Consumer restores at boot with nothing to catch a throw, and the checkpoint that would clear it is never written, so every builder would crash-loop. `fill()` always stores and forwards the record's flame, then folds the record into the stats only when its crumb is empty or differs from `$counted`, and records the crumb after folding. A replay of the counted record is therefore forwarded again and counted once. An empty crumb matches nothing, so a record the reader stamped no ID on is always counted.

`Request_Builder_Node` needs no crumb: every firehose line carries its request's sequence number `n`, and a line below the request's expected number is dropped as `INFO: duplicate message`, so a replayed line joins nothing twice.

*What it costs:* the crumb in the flame builder's checkpoint, beside the span it carries, and one comparison per record; a replayed record's flame is written to the flames partition a second time.

*Alternatives rejected:* counting the replay twice, which is what Tachikoma's `Nodes/Consumer.pm` does: its `commit_offset` records the lowest in-flight offset beside a cache that has already folded the messages past it, so a restart replays those and folds them again, and here every stop would inflate the counts, the sums and the leaderboards by the record in flight ([decision 2](#decision-2-sums-not-means)); and staging each record's fold until its `deferring()` bracket closed cleanly, which puts a second accumulator layer and a merge on every record to guard against a stop that lands once per worker life, where one string comparison answers the same question.

**Revisit if** a stop can leave the cursor more than one record behind the fold — a reader that folds a batch before it advances, or a checkpoint that commits a cursor older than the state beside it — since a single crumb then names only the last of several records the successor would count again.

## Decision 32: A builder tells its upkeep on its own worker record, and every narration write is a guarded forward

`Flame_Builder_Node` and `Request_Builder_Node` tell their routine writes and upkeep on the worker's own request record, as `keep`-marked spans and points through the [`Narration`](../includes/trait-narration.php) trait, and never through stderr, which feeds the Error Log. The Error Log therefore hears only faults. Everything a line says rides its `m`, because the stored record keeps an entry's `m` and drops every other producer field. Counters accumulate per name in the node's tally, and writing that name's line resets them.

Each narration line is a write to the firehose Partition, and a Partition write is a point where the worker's cooperative stop can rise. A snapshot node therefore sends every narration line through `guarded()` ([substrate ADR-14](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-14-cooperative-stop-propagates-through-broad-catches)), as it sends any downstream forward: inside the `deferring()` bracket of the message or tick in progress, the stop waits for that work to finish and then leaves clean. `spanned()` guards its `(start)` and its `(complete)` apart, through `Log_Manager::timed()`'s `$write`, inside a `deferring()` bracket of its own that is itself one guarded forward, so a stop the start raises still runs the step and closes its span — where a message's or tick's bracket is open it then waits for that work, and where none is, as in the restore `Consumer_Node::init_position()` runs before the first message, it leaves once the span has closed. Unguarded, a line raises the stop in the middle of the work it narrates, and the work after it never runs. An eviction or a fold tells no line of its own, only a count the next `requests writes` rollup carries, because both track visitor traffic; and a `requests checkpoint` is told only when its carry may have moved since the last one told. A request builder's narration, read back off the firehose, is folded but counts as neither a line nor a move: counted, a builder reading its own record, or two reading each other's, would tell for ever. The trait declares `guarded()` abstract, so it composes only with the substrate's `Deferred_Clean_Stop`.

*What it costs:* a closure per narration line, and one per span half, where a record is started; nothing where none is, because with no started logger no line is built.

*Alternatives rejected:* stderr diagnostics, which never stop a worker, since `Event_Framework::stop_check()` does nothing inside a stderr write, but put every routine line in the Error Log beside the faults; and one `guarded()` around a whole `timed()` span, which skips the step itself when its start write raises.

**Revisit if** narration grows past a few hundred lines per worker lifetime, or an operator needs routine lines in the Error Log.

## Decision 34: Stats live in SQLite, and nothing repairs a loss

Memcache evicts, and every mechanism the stats grew there existed to survive that: the durable mirror of decisions [10](#decision-10-the-durable-mirror-writes-a-bucket-once-when-it-closes-under-a-key-that-carries-no-install-scope) and [11](#decision-11-the-open-bucket-is-durable-in-the-offsetlog-until-it-closes-and-the-carry-is-capped), its index format, the sweep of [decision 33](#decision-33-no-dashboard-reads-the-mirror-the-flame-builder-sweeps-it-back-into-memcache), held frames, restore lifetimes, and the probe that re-ranked lists memcache had dropped. Each review of that machinery found holes in the last round's repair. So the stats live on stores that lose nothing a reader reads — the nine Ledgers of [decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them), which delete a row only when its segment ages out or a flush empties the Ledger, and the `flame-stats:url` Table, which keeps a blob for its TTL — and the repairs went with the loss.

**The topology declares them.** [`flame-builder.tsl`](../topologies/flame-builder.tsl) declares the nine Ledgers, one file each, `{base}/ledgers/{name}.sqlite`, and the `flame-stats:url` Table, `evlog:p<partition>` on the substrate's `sqlite` backend ([substrate ADR-24](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-24-a-tables-backend-is-chosen-per-table)), one file per partition, `{base}/tables/flame-stats:url.p{N}.sqlite`, all on the one host's local filesystem the substrate already requires. `performance` and `complete` inherit the declarations through their includes, so writer and reader read one declaration, and a topology whose stores cannot open, `pdo_sqlite` missing among the causes, fails at load, naming the store.

**The builder appends a span at a time.** [decision 37](#decision-37-the-span-settles-at-the-interval-checkpoint-and-every-other-checkpoint-carries-it) settles the span at the Consumer's interval checkpoint: one `APPEND` per Ledger holding rows, then the span's URL blobs, `WRITE_BATCH_KEYS` (500) to an `MSET`, through the substrate's `Table_Client`. Only the servers `stats:names` holds are read first ([decision 3](#decision-3-stats-fail-soft)), and nothing waits in memory for a bucket to close.

**Readers mount for the request.** A `performance` verb mounts the Ledgers it reads through `Bootstrap::mount_ledger()`, and for `dump_url` and the `url:` briefs every partition's url Table through `Bootstrap::mount_table()`, read-only and in-process, and a mount lives for the rest of the request, so a later verb in the same POST reads through it ([substrate ADR-23](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-23-a-request-carries-no-authority-of-its-own)). That is sound only because every row those stores hold is data a READ caller may already read: each is an aggregate of request records and their flames, which `dump_request`, `grep_requests` and the substrate's firehose stream hand a READ caller whole, and each store surfaces through a READ verb besides — the URL rows, the names and the words through `urls`; the totals, dimensions, categories and leaderboard through `overview`; a URL's dimensions and categories through `dump_url` and `url_breakdown`; and the blob through `dump_url`. A Ledger no writer has made a file for yet reads empty. [decision 3](#decision-3-stats-fail-soft) says what a store that will not open or answer costs.

**Deleted with it:** the `flame-stats:partition` mirror, its `stats-index` format and the four `stats_mirror_*` config keys; the sweep; held frames; the probe of lost lists and records; the site-hour refold; the `set_stats_target` and `set_flame_topn` verbs; `GET_STATS`' `mirror_held_frames` and `mirror_held_bytes`; and with the Ledgers, the `flame-stats:aggregate` and `flame-stats:url-fine` Tables and every key decisions [17](#decision-17-the-url-index-is-stored-at-two-resolutions-and-the-coarse-one-is-derived), [30](#decision-30-every-memcache-value-is-one-servers-carries-nothing-its-key-implies-and-fits-one-item) and [35](#decision-35-a-chart-hour-is-one-key-holding-its-twelve-five-minute-slots) describe.

**Migration:** none, and the stats start empty. Nothing reads the memcache keys or the retired Tables' files; the upgrade guide names the files an operator deletes. `wp nodes memcache flush` rotates the salt and restarts every worker and never touches the stats; `wp nodes tables flush` is what empties them ([decision 5](#decision-5-a-stats-schema-change-is-migrated-by-the-tables-flush-and-by-nothing-in-this-code)).

*What it costs:* the `pdo_sqlite` extension on every host running the flame builder; disk under `{base}/ledgers` and `{base}/tables`; a stats reader bound to the flame builder's host and filesystem; and a substrate floor at the release that carries the Ledger and the reads the stats pages send.

*Alternatives rejected:* memcache with a better repair, each round of which found holes in the last; the substrate's `wpdb` arm, durable and shared across hosts, which puts every settle's rows on the site's database and is sized for low-volume Tables; and a migrator from memcache, which would carry an evictable store's gaps into a durable one.

**Revisit if** a stats reader must run on a host other than the flame builder's.

## Decision 35: A chart hour is one key holding its twelve five-minute slots

**Superseded by [decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them).** This record stored each chart hour as one key holding its twelve five-minute slots, so a chart read 25 hour keys to draw the 288 slots of the last 24 hours, and kept the leaderboard at one sum an hour. Under decision 36 every row sits at its own five-minute `t`, so a chart is one `SUM` over its 288 buckets, by bucket, and the leaderboard is read over the same buckets ([decision 15](#decision-15-the-urls-verb-owns-every-url-set-fact-overview-owns-the-site)).

## Decision 36: Stats are write-once rows, one Ledger file each, and every read aggregates them

The stats are rows written once, in the substrate's Ledgers ([substrate ADR-27](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-27-a-ledger-is-one-sqlite-file-every-partition-writes-and-its-rows-age-out-by-the-segment)), and every total a dashboard shows is an aggregate the Ledger computes over them when asked. [decision 1](#decision-1-the-stats-schema-nine-ledgers-a-key-per-scope) lists the rows; this record is how they are stored, read and aged.

**One shared file per Ledger.** [`flame-builder.tsl`](../topologies/flame-builder.tsl) declares nine, and every partition's flame builder declaring one appends to its one file, `{base}/ledgers/{name}.sqlite`; SQLite serializes the writers, each `APPEND` one transaction that another partition waits out for up to `Ledger_Node::BUSY_TIMEOUT_MS` (5 s). A reader opens that one file per Ledger, whatever the partition count or the window, and merges nothing across partitions.

**Rows are write-once deltas.** A settle appends what its span folded ([decision 37](#decision-37-the-span-settles-at-the-interval-checkpoint-and-every-other-checkpoint-carries-it)) as rows keyed `( t, k, x, w, s )`: `t` the bucket, `k` the scope, `x` the member, `w` the partition that wrote the row and `s` a sequence of that writer's, one per row, so two deltas never collide. No row is updated and none is read before a write: a second row for one `( t, k, x )` is one more delta, one per settle that saw it, about ten a partition in a five-minute bucket, and every read adds them. A late record is an ordinary delta at its own `t` ([decision 19](#decision-19-a-request-is-filed-in-the-bucket-it-finished-in)).

**Reads aggregate the rows.** Every read names a window, `[ from, to )`, and the keys it asks, and the Ledger aggregates in SQL, each column by the aggregate its declaration names — `sum` by default, `min_ms` a `min`, the other extremes a `max` — so no reader sums a scope in PHP ([decision 6](#decision-6-a-read-is-one-ask-per-store-it-spans)). `SUM` answers each `( k, x )`, by bucket for a chart, or each key whole with `group: 'k'` for a header's totals. `TOP` ranks a scope's members by a column, by `x`, or by the ratio of two sums, and answers the page and the set's size from one snapshot ([decision 28](#decision-28-a-url-page-ranks-in-the-ledger-and-a-search-reads-the-word-index)). `MEMBERS` lists a key's distinct members, up to a limit. A `min` or `max` never measured is null, which its aggregate skips and a `TOP` ranks last ([decision 24](#decision-24-a-duration-nobody-measured-is-not-a-timing-sample)). `positive_each_t` filters each stored `( t, k, x )` before any grouping, which is what `errors_only` is.

**Retention is by segment.** Every Ledger is declared with hour-long segments, `<eln:ledger_segments>` of them: the retention window in hours, rounded up, and never fewer than `Stats_Store::MIN_SEGMENTS` (25), the 24 hours a chart draws and the hour it is in. The Router tick deletes whole segments off the old edge of the file once the wall clock passes them, and an `APPEND` row already past the lifespan drops and is counted `past the lifespan` on the `stats writes` line. No row carries a TTL and nothing prunes by hand.

**Search is a set.** `stats:search` declares no columns, so it is a set keyed `( t, k, x )`, a word and a URL, written with `INSERT OR IGNORE`. Every settle files each word of each URL it saw (`Stats_Store::url_words()`) at the start of the hour it saw the URL in, so a word filed again that hour is stored once and a URL seen in a later hour is filed there again, searchable as long as its rows are read. A search reads it with `MEMBERS`, one a word, asking at most `URL_SEARCH_MAX` (5,000) members, which the Ledger stops reading past, so a common word costs its limit and never its every URL; [decision 28](#decision-28-a-url-page-ranks-in-the-ledger-and-a-search-reads-the-word-index) says how the words combine. `stats:names` is the other set: a hash's URL and each family's servers, filed the same way.

**Names are read from the hour.** A name or a word is filed at its hour's start, so a read of either Ledger opens on the hour its window's `from` falls in (`Stats_Store::members_of()`): a URL seen at 10:40 is filed at 10:00, and a window opening at 10:15 still finds it.

**The servers are capped.** `server_name` is the client's Host header under Apache's default `UseCanonicalName Off`, so the servers a window names are visitor input. A settle reads the servers `stats:names` holds over the last 24 hours and files each server of its span past `Stats_Store::MAX_SERVER_VALUES` (128) under the `Other` server, in every per-server key and on the `server` dimension (`Flame_Builder_Node::admitted()`). `Other` holds no slot. The ceiling sits far above any fleet in evidence, so a real fleet keeps every spoke by name, and the tail FOLDS rather than drops, keeping every total exact. Two partitions settling at once each admit up to the room they read, which the next settle holds to. A read the names Ledger does not answer fails the settle rather than admitting against nothing ([decision 3](#decision-3-stats-fail-soft)). `Stats_Store::servers()` answers false past `SERVERS_READ_MAX` (250), the servers a site page's `TOP` can name at two keys each; a site page refuses then, asking for a server, and a settle reads it as no room. It answers null when the Ledger does not answer, which a page reads as `provisional` and a settle as a failure.

*What it costs:* every read aggregates its window's rows, and nothing ranks at write time, so a miss of the page cache pays a `TOP` over the window; the `ua`, `ja4` and `from` dimensions store every value they see, uncapped, so a chart of one carries every value its window holds; a Ledger reads only on the flame builder's host and needs `pdo_sqlite`; and a hard crash between a settle's appends and the checkpoint that commits them appends that span twice ([decision 37](#decision-37-the-span-settles-at-the-interval-checkpoint-and-every-other-checkpoint-carries-it)).

*Alternatives rejected:* the memcache-keyed values of [decision 17](#decision-17-the-url-index-is-stored-at-two-resolutions-and-the-coarse-one-is-derived), [decision 30](#decision-30-every-memcache-value-is-one-servers-carries-nothing-its-key-implies-and-fits-one-item) and [decision 35](#decision-35-a-chart-hour-is-one-key-holding-its-twelve-five-minute-slots), which read, merged and rewrote open buckets and hour keys on every flush and paid a repair for every eviction; one `sqlite` Table per partition, which every reader opened and merged; a file per partition per segment, which a day's read at four partitions opens 96 of; and ranked lists written per bucket, which rank a window's sums only approximately.

**Revisit if** a read cannot be expressed as `SUM`, `TOP` or `MEMBERS` over the window, or if a dimension's values outgrow what a read of the window can aggregate in its budget, which a count per settle of the values already seen that hour would cap.

## Decision 37: The span settles at the interval checkpoint, and every other checkpoint carries it

What `Flame_Builder_Node` has folded since it last wrote is its SPAN: `$pending`, one accumulator per five-minute bucket, and `$url_acc`, each URL's flame tree and profile sums as the span has folded them onto the stored blob. The span is written at one moment only, so every Ledger row is one span's delta ([decision 36](#decision-36-stats-are-write-once-rows-one-ledger-file-each-and-every-read-aggregates-them)).

**The interval checkpoint settles it.** Once a `Consumer_Node::CHECKPOINT_INTERVAL_S` (30 s) while its cursor moves, the requests Consumer writes its interval checkpoint, and before it saves the builder's state it calls `settle()`, inside its uninterruptible save. `settle()` appends the span's rows, one `APPEND` a Ledger, writes its URL blobs, and empties both, so `save_state()` then returns `{ counted, span: [] }` and the frame carries no stats. A span is one interval of folds when every settle lands, and its rows land together in the newest few `t` ranges of each Ledger.

**Every other checkpoint carries it.** A clean stop's frame, a crawl's per-record one and a boot recommit write nothing: `save_state()` carries the span unsettled beside [decision 31](#decision-31-a-snapshot-nodes-replay-is-idempotent-by-the-last-folded-crumb)'s crumb, `pending` whole and the URL trees under `CARRY_URL_BYTES` (16 MiB, half the checkpoint line's 32 MiB), and the successor's `restore_state()` takes it back, so its next interval checkpoint settles it once. A tree past the budget is left out of the carry and its URL keeps the blob it last settled, told once a span. A span restored onto an idle log is not settled until a record moves the cursor, because a settle whose frame never commits would leave the old frame carrying the span too, and the next restart would append it again. A stop therefore costs the span no write, and a crawl no longer writes a record at a time.

**A hostless record dead-letters at the fold's intake.** Every logged URL carries its host, which the record's server is read from. `fold_record()` reads it first (`Stats_Store::server_of()`), before any fold, and a URL with no host throws there, so the Consumer quarantines that one record under its dead-letter directory ([substrate ADR-12](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-12-dead-letter-poison--crash-lifecycle)), replayable with `wp nodes ingest`, and reads on. A settle never meets one, so no record can fail a 30-second span.

**A settle that fails is carried, not replayed.** A throw out of `settle()` — a Ledger the names read found silent ([decision 3](#decision-3-stats-fail-soft)), or any store failure that raises — leaves the Consumer's timer, ends the event loop, and reaches the worker's shutdown, which is not a fatal one: `shutdown_handoff()` hands every reader its cursor, and the requests Consumer's `checkpoint_shutdown()` commits a graceful frame at the cursor, carrying the unsettled span. The worker respawns, restores the span, and settles it at its next interval checkpoint. Nothing is read again.

*What it costs:* a hard crash between a settle's appends and the frame that commits them appends that span twice, because the successor restores the span the previous frame carried and settles it again; the replay's double count is the one the CHANGELOG's Known section accepts. A names Ledger that keeps refusing respawns the flame-builder worker about once a `CHECKPOINT_INTERVAL_S`, 30 s, and `pending` grows across those respawns, since each carries the last span and folds another interval onto it: only the URL trees are bounded, by `CARRY_URL_BYTES`. A restart on an idle log leaves its restored span unread until traffic resumes, up to one interval of lag for the readers. And the offsetlog carries up to one span of buckets and trees on every non-interval frame.

*Alternatives rejected:* settling at every checkpoint, which makes a crawl append a record at a time; holding each bucket until it closes, which a reader cannot see until then and a restart must carry whole ([substrate ADR-27](https://github.com/Automattic/newspack-nodes/blob/main/docs/architecture-decisions.md#adr-27-a-ledger-is-one-sqlite-file-every-partition-writes-and-its-rows-age-out-by-the-segment)); refusing a hostless record at the request builder, which drops its URL and files the traffic under no server; and throwing at the settle, which lets one old record fail every span it lands in.

**Revisit if** a checkpoint other than the interval one must write; if the trees a span folds approach `CARRY_URL_BYTES`, so that a carry routinely leaves URLs out; or if a store refusing for longer than a few intervals is seen, since `pending` then grows without a bound and the checkpoint line, 32 MiB, becomes the limit.
